## The Morning After

There is a particular silence that settles over a house after a loss. The kettle still works. The clock on the wall still ticks. People still ring the doorbell with casseroles. But something in the air has changed shape, and you find yourself moving through the rooms as if you are underwater — slow, muffled, half-real.

Grief is not a single feeling. It is a country. And the country has weather. Some days you are functional. Some days a song on the radio undoes you in the supermarket. Some days you forget for a moment, and the remembering is its own small fresh wound.

Scripture does not flinch from any of this. It does not tell you to be brave. It does not give you a verse to tape on the fridge that will make it stop hurting. The Bible is, instead, full of grieving people — ordinary, faithful, wrecked people — and the God who sat down beside them in the ashes.

## Job in the Ashes

When Job lost his children, his health, and almost everything he owned, his friends came. And the most healing thing they ever did, they did first.

> So they sat down with him upon the ground seven days and seven nights, and none spake a word unto him: for they saw that his grief was very great.
>
> Job 2:13

Seven days of silence. No platitudes. No theology. No hurried scripture. Just presence. It was only when they began to *speak* that they began to wound him.

There is a lesson in this for any of us tempted to fix grief — our own or anyone else's. The first ministry of grief is presence. The Lord Himself often comes that way: not with a torrent of explanations, but as a quiet weight beside you on the ground.

## Jesus Wept

Of all the verses in the Bible, the shortest may be the most pastoral.

> Jesus wept.
>
> John 11:35

He was about to raise Lazarus from the dead. He knew that. He knew Mary and Martha would have their brother back within minutes. And still — *still* — He stood at the tomb and wept.

This matters more than we often realise. The God who knew the end of the story still honoured the grief in the middle of it. He did not say to Mary, *don't cry, I'm about to fix this*. He cried with her first. The miracle came after the tears, not instead of them.

If anyone ever tells you that grief is a sign of weak faith, point them to John 11. The Lord of Life Himself wept at a grave.

## A God Near to the Broken

The Psalms are explicit: there is a particular nearness God reserves for those whose hearts are in pieces.

> The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit.
>
> Psalm 34:18

> He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds.
>
> Psalm 147:3

Read those verses slowly. *Nigh*, not far. *Healeth*, in the present tense. *Bindeth up* — the language of a careful hand on a wound. This is not a God who watches grief from a distance. This is a God who comes close, sleeves rolled up, ready for the slow work of repair.

> For thou hast delivered my soul from death, mine eyes from tears, and my feet from falling.
>
> Psalm 116:8

The Lord notices tears. Scripture even says He gathers them.

> Put thou my tears into thy bottle: are they not in thy book?
>
> Psalm 56:8

Whatever you have wept this year — at night, in the car, in the bathroom with the tap running so no one would hear — none of it has been wasted. None of it has been unseen.

## Mourning Is Not Hopelessness

When Paul wrote to a young church grieving its dead, he did not tell them to stop grieving. He told them to grieve differently from the world.

> But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope.
>
> 1 Thessalonians 4:13

*Sorrow not, even as others which have no hope.* The verse does not say *do not sorrow*. It assumes we will. It teaches us *how*. Christian grief is real grief, with the door of resurrection left open at the back of the room. We weep, and we weep honestly. But the weeping is not the last word.

> For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first.
>
> 1 Thessalonians 4:16

The promise is not that grief will be denied. The promise is that death will be undone.

## The Permission to Lament

Western Christianity often does not know what to do with sorrow. We rush past Good Friday to get to Easter. We post praise songs over private aches. But the Bible is unhurried. It gives us a whole book — Lamentations — for the work of weeping aloud.

> Mine eye runneth down with rivers of water for the destruction of the daughter of my people.
>
> Lamentations 3:48

> Remembering mine affliction and my misery, the wormwood and the gall.
> My soul hath them still in remembrance, and is humbled in me.
>
> Lamentations 3:19-20

Jeremiah does not skip past the bitterness. He names it. *Wormwood and gall.* And then, only after the long honest naming, comes one of the most beloved promises in scripture:

> It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not.
> They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.
>
> Lamentations 3:22-23

Notice the order. The mercy does not erase the wormwood. It comes *through* it. *New every morning* — meaning that yesterday's grace was not meant to carry today's grief. Today's mercy is for today.

If you woke this morning and felt the weight of loss again, that is not because you lacked faith. That is because today is its own day. And there is mercy made new for it.

## When the Grief Is Old

Sometimes the loss is not recent. Sometimes the funeral was years ago, and you thought you had moved on, and then a smell or a date or a stranger's laugh cracks it open again. This is normal. This is grief doing what grief does. The Bible never gives us a timetable.

David grieved his son Absalom even after years of estrangement and rebellion. The cry remains one of the rawest in scripture:

> O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son!
>
> 2 Samuel 18:33

David is the king. He is on the verge of military victory. And he stops everything to weep for the boy he could not save. Scripture records it without reproach. There is nothing unspiritual about a parent grieving a child, no matter what that child became, no matter how long ago it was.

## A Time for Everything

Ecclesiastes refuses the lie that grief is a phase to be optimised through.

> To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven:
> A time to be born, and a time to die;
> ...
> A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance.
>
> Ecclesiastes 3:1-4

Notice: weeping and laughing are equally on the list. Mourning is not failure. It is *time*. And the same God who appointed the time to laugh appointed the time to mourn. He has space for both seasons in the same heart.

## The Promise That Outlasts the Grave

For the Christian, grief is real, but it is not eternal. The last chapter of scripture makes a promise that no other book in the world dares make:

> And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away.
>
> Revelation 21:4

The verb is intimate. *Wipe away*. Not declare them irrelevant. Not dismiss them. Wipe them — finger to cheek, the way a parent does to a child. Every tear, He has counted, and every tear, He will personally undo.

This is not a promise that you will forget. It is the promise that what was lost will be made new — and that the One who walks with you through the valley today will, on the other side, gather you to a feast where no chair is empty.

## A Word for the One Who Is Tired of Being Strong

Maybe you have been the one holding the family together. The one answering the door. The one writing the thank-you notes. And tonight, with no one watching, you are very, very tired.

You can come and put it down. The Lord does not ask you to be composed. He does not even ask you to know what to pray.

> Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.
>
> Matthew 11:28

Bring the loss. Bring the name. Bring the photograph and the empty side of the bed and the dread of the next first holiday. Bring it to the One who wept at a tomb knowing the resurrection was minutes away — and who weeps with you still, knowing the resurrection is coming for you, too.

## An Invitation

Grief is not something to get through. It is something to walk through, slowly, with the Shepherd who promised to walk it with you.

> Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me.
>
> Psalm 23:4

You may walk this valley a long time. But you will not walk it alone. And one morning — His morning — you will look up and find that the shadow has lifted, and the One who wept beside you is the One who has carried you home.
